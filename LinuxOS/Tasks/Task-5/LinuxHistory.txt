Linux, an open-source operating system, was originally developed by Linus Torvalds in 1991. Torvalds, then a student at the University of
Helsinki, aimed to create a free and open alternative to proprietary Unix systems for personal computers. He began by writing the Linux
kernel, which is the core component of an operating system that manages system resources and hardware interaction.

The early Linux kernel was designed to work alongside software from the **GNU Project**, founded by Richard Stallman in 1983. GNU’s goal was
to create a free operating system, and although it developed many important components like compilers and utilities, it lacked a kernel.
When Torvalds released the Linux kernel, it was combined with the existing GNU tools to form what’s now commonly referred to as **GNU
/Linux**.

The Linux system grew in popularity due to its open-source nature, meaning that anyone could view, modify, and redistribute the code. Over
time, this collaboration between developers across the globe transformed Linux into one of the most widely used operating systems,
particularly in server environments and embedded systems.

The GNU/Linux philosophy is based on the ideals of **free software** as articulated by Richard Stallman. At the heart of this philosophy are
the **Four Freedoms** of free software:
1. **Freedom to run the software** for any purpose.
2. **Freedom to study and modify the software** (access to source code).
3. **Freedom to share the software** with others.
4. **Freedom to distribute modified versions** of the software.

This focus on user autonomy, openness, and community-driven development sets GNU/Linux apart from proprietary operating systems, promoting
an ecosystem where software is transparent, customizable, and open to improvement by anyone. The combination of the Linux kernel and the GNU
tools has led to a powerful, flexible operating system that has become ubiquitous in both consumer and enterprise environments.
